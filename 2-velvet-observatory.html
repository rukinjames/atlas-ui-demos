What a scoop shop actually pays per transaction

Every processor quotes the same headline: two point six percent plus ten cents. The number that matters to a shop selling four-dollar cones is not the percentage — it is the fixed cent charge, because it lands on every single sale regardless of size.

At an average ticket of $4.20, ten cents of fixed cost is another 2.4 percent on top of the quoted rate. A shop doing 180 tickets on a Saturday pays $18 in flat fees before the percentage is counted. Over a St. Louis summer, the fixed leg alone runs past $1,400.

Ask for the interchange-plus sheet. If the sales rep will not send one, the blended rate is hiding something.
Section 3 · fees you can negotiate

Hardware is the second trap. A countertop terminal leased at $39 a month costs more over two years than buying the same unit outright, and the lease rarely covers the card reader you will actually use at a farmers' market or a dorm-lobby pop-up.

For a shop with one register, one iPad, and a Saturday line out the door, the practical questions are narrow: does it work offline when the dorm Wi-Fi drops, does it split tips fairly across a shift, and can a new hire learn it in ten minutes without a manual.

Offline mode is where the cheap tiers fail. Two of the three systems compared here queue card payments locally and settle when the connection returns; the third refuses the sale outright, which on a 90-degree Saturday means a walked customer and a melted cone.
